Design philosophy

THE
PRINCIPLES

01
Hierarchy & Contrast
Every composition communicates a reading order. I use scale, weight, and colour contrast deliberately. The goal is to guide the viewer's eye to the message before they're aware of being guided.
02
User-Centred Thinking
In UI/UX, aesthetics serve function. I map user journeys, identify friction, and prototype iteratively, so every interface feels effortless, not just attractive.
03
Consistency & Systems
Scalable design requires repeatable logic. I build component libraries and visual systems so work stays cohesive whether it's a social post, an app screen, or a broadcast overlay.
04
Intentional Emotion
Colour psychology, typographic mood, and spatial rhythm shape how an audience feels. I make these choices deliberately, whether the work needs to command, calm, or excite.

Case Study, Mobile App Design

VIBELY

2025
Figma Mobile Events UX Research Concept Project

The problem

FRAGMENTED
EVENT DISCOVERY

Most people I talked to in Kingston and Montego Bay find out about parties and concerts the same way: a WhatsApp forward, three days late, missing half the details. I ran a short survey of about 20 people, mostly 18 to 35, and the same complaint kept coming up. They'd skipped something they actually wanted to go to, just because nobody told them in time.

Vibely is my answer to that: one place to see what's on nearby, who else is going, and a way to grab a ticket without leaving the app. Dark by default and mobile only, built for someone scrolling at 11pm trying to decide where the night is headed.

01

The feed comes first

My first pass put browse categories right on the home screen, concerts, food, and nightlife. Almost nobody who tested it tapped into them first; they wanted to scroll. So the home screen leads with a feed of what's happening nearby this week, sorted by date. Categories are still there, just one tap deeper instead of the front door.

02

Trust had to be designed in

Ticket scams come up constantly in conversations about events here, so the event card carries a verified-promoter badge and a real attendee count before anyone taps through. It's a small detail on screen, but it was the one piece of feedback that came up unprompted in almost every usability check.

03

One screen, not five

The checkout flow went through three versions before landing on this one. Date, ticket type, and payment all live on a single screen with no multi-step wizard. Anyone who's tried to buy a concert ticket on a slow connection at a bus stop will understand why that mattered.

Vibely is still a concept project. There's no real payment processor wired up, and the promoter side of the app (the dashboard for posting events) only exists as a sketch right now. That's next if I keep building it out.

Case Study, Mobile App Design

FIXUPJA

2025
Figma Mobile Services UX Flow Concept Project

The problem

FINDING HELP IS
WORD OF MOUTH

Jamaica has a long list of small infrastructure problems , including potholes, broken streetlights, and blocked drains, with no real system to report them. What kept coming up, both in conversation and in how people post about it online, is that most complaints end up on Twitter or Facebook instead of in front of anyone who can actually fix it. Noise, but no action.

FixUpJa is built around one loop: someone spots a problem, snaps a photo with a GPS pin, and the report routes to the right government body, whether NWA, KSAC, or the parish council, automatically. The harder part was designing for three completely different people inside one app.

→

→

#2B4D18
#7A7A0D

01

A map people can actually read

Red for new, yellow for in progress, green for fixed. Sounds obvious, but most civic apps bury status behind a list. Putting it on the map first means a citizen can see at a glance whether their report disappeared into a void or is actually being worked on.

02

Privacy came before the camera

Taking photos of public infrastructure in Jamaica isn't always simple. Faces, plates, and house numbers end up in frame by accident. Before anyone can submit a report, there's a short walkthrough on what to avoid and a blur tool for anything that slips through. It's the first thing a new user sees, on purpose.

03

The crew's screen had to be the simplest

Citizens and government admins can handle some complexity. Crews working with their hands and a phone in a back pocket can't. Their view strips down to a job list, a before/after photo upload, and a close-out button. Nothing else competing for attention.

This is a concept project, not a deployed product. The email-routing system that would send reports to NWA or KSAC is designed but not actually connected to anything live. That would need sign-off I don't have. If an agency ever wanted to pilot something like this, that's the piece I'd want to test first.

Self-Initiated, Web Design & Brand

BLAZE
BARBER
SHOP

2026
Framer Web Design Branding Booking UX Concept Project

The problem

INVISIBLE
IN A
DIGITAL AGE

Most local barbershops I see around Montego Bay run entirely on word of mouth and a pinned location, with no website, nothing to look up before showing up. That gap is what got me curious: what would it look like if a barbershop's site took itself as seriously as the actual haircuts?

Blaze Barbershop isn't a real business. It's a concept I built from scratch, with every detail invented. The name, branding, pricing, and team are all made up, created to explore what a confident, no-fluff grooming brand could look like online.

01

Dark, not generic

Most barbershop sites default to white backgrounds and stock photos of scissors. Going dark with a gold accent was the one move that made it look like nobody else's, premium without trying too hard to say the word.

02

Show the price before they ask

Hiding prices creates hesitation, especially for someone who's never been in before. Every service card shows a starting price up front, so there are no surprises and no awkward question at the chair.

03

People book a barber, not a shop

Giving each barber a name, a photo, and a specialty, whether fades, beard work, or classic cuts, lets someone form a preference before they ever walk in. Small detail, does a lot of work.

There's a live build at the link below, but it's a concept through and through, with no real booking backend and no actual barbershop behind it. Built to explore the idea, not to launch a business.

VOYAGE
VACATIONS

2026
Framer Web Design Luxury Travel Concept Project

The problem

LOOKING
BUDGET IN A
LUXURY MARKET

Most travel sites in the luxury space don't actually look luxury. They look like every other booking platform, just with nicer photos. I wanted to see if I could design something that felt like the trip had already started the moment you landed on the page, before a single price was mentioned.

Voyagé isn't a real travel agency. It's a concept built around what I think a premium travel brand's site should feel like: unhurried, photo-led, confident enough to leave things out. Built in Framer for the scroll animations and fast iteration.

01

Restraint is the luxury signal

No price tables, no bullet lists, no cluttered nav. Luxury brands don't compete for attention. They assume they already have it. The page is mostly white space and one photograph at a time.

02

Let the photography do the selling

A villa in the Maldives doesn't need a paragraph under it. Every destination gets full-bleed space and minimal copy , just enough to set the scene, not explain it.

03

"Book Now" means talk to us, not checkout

There's no price anywhere on the homepage. The CTA invites a conversation instead of a transaction, which fits how luxury travel sales actually tend to work, through advisors, not carts.

Like Blaze, this one's speculative. The destinations, pricing, and testimonials are invented to make the concept feel real, not pulled from an actual agency I worked with.
